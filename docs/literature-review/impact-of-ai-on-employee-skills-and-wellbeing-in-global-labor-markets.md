# The Impact of Artificial Intelligence on Employees' Skills and Well-being in Global Labor Markets: A Systematic Review

**Authors:** Romana Emilia Cramarenco, Monica Ioana Burcă-Voicu, Dan-Cristian Dabija  
**Year:** 2023  
**Published in:** Oeconomia Copernicana, Volume 14, Issue 3, pp. 731–767 — DOI: 10.24136/oc.2023.022 (Funded by Romanian Ministry of Education and Research, project PN-III-P1-1.1-TE-2021-0795)

---

## Problem Statement

The COVID-19 pandemic did not create the tension between AI adoption and workforce skills — but it accelerated it dramatically. Organizations deployed AI-powered tools rapidly during 2020–2023, compressing digital transformation timelines from years to months. Workers who lacked the necessary digital skills found themselves facing sudden skill obsolescence, while those with the right competencies became more valuable. What remains unclear is how this transformation is distributed across skill types and how it is affecting employee well-being beyond the professional dimension.

Prior research had studied either AI's impact on skills or AI's impact on well-being — but not both simultaneously, and not through the integrated lens of the pandemic period. This review addresses that gap by jointly analyzing how AI adoption affected the professional dimension (upskilling and reskilling across five skill categories) and the personal dimension (five components of occupational well-being) using two complementary theoretical frameworks.

## Key Contributions & Objectives

1. The first systematic review to jointly examine AI's impact on employee skills and employee well-being through a unified PRISMA framework — covering March 2020 to May 2023 (WHO-declared COVID-19 pandemic period).
2. A structured dual-framework analysis: Livingstone et al.'s (2022) four-component digital skills model (Technical, Information, Communication, Content Creation) combined with Van Horn et al.'s (2004) five-dimension occupational well-being model (Cognitive, Social, Professional, Affective, Psychosomatic).
3. Bibliometric network analysis using VOSviewer (v1.6.19) mapping co-occurrence, citation, bibliographic coupling, and co-authorship across the retrieved literature.
4. Identification of a critical research gap: content creation (digital skills) and psychosomatic well-being are almost entirely absent from the literature despite being directly impacted by AI adoption — future research directions are formulated accordingly.
5. Synthesis of 15 skills-related and 35 well-being-related articles into a structured evidence base with five future research questions for each dimension.

## Methodology / Theory / Framework

The review follows Khan et al.'s (2023) five-step systematic methodology combined with the PRISMA flowchart. Papers were sourced from Scopus and Web of Science, restricted to full-text English articles published between March 2020 and May 2023. Primary search terms: "employees," "artificial intelligence," "AI." Secondary terms covered reskilling, upskilling, digital skills, soft and hard skills, and all five Van Horn well-being dimensions. VOSviewer was used for bibliometric network construction. Two theoretical frameworks structure the analysis: Livingstone et al.'s digital skills model organizes the skills dimension into Technical, Information, Communication, and Content Creation; Van Horn et al.'s occupational well-being model organizes the personal dimension into Cognitive, Social, Professional, Affective, and Psychosomatic components.

## Software Tools / Setup Details

- Databases: Scopus (484 initial records) and Web of Science (418 initial records)
- VOSviewer v1.6.19 for bibliometric network visualization
- PRISMA 2020 flowchart for selection reporting
- Theoretical frameworks: Van Horn et al. (2004) occupational well-being model; Livingstone et al. (2022) four-component digital skills model

## Test / Experiment Analysis

PRISMA funnel: 902 initial papers (484 from Scopus, 418 from WoS), 337 duplicates removed, 639 screened. Final included papers: 103 for the skills dimension (after further de-duplication), 35 for the well-being dimension. Analyzed in depth: 15 skills-related articles and 35 well-being articles.

Skills dimension findings: 11 of 15 articles focused on reskilling; only 4 addressed upskilling. 14 of 15 examined digital skills; 13 examined hard skills; 8 examined soft skills. Against Livingstone's framework: 9 of 11 digital skills papers concentrated on Technical skills; 5 on Information processing; only 1 examined Content Creation; only 2 covered three of four components. Content creation (using AI to produce new digital outputs) is grossly underrepresented.

Well-being dimension findings: Cognitive (F1): 2 papers; Social (F2): 9 papers; Professional (F3): 0 papers; Affective (F4): 1 paper; Psychosomatic (F5): 0 papers. Both Professional well-being (job satisfaction, organizational commitment) and Psychosomatic well-being (physical symptoms, fatigue, stress) are entirely unaddressed. The literature heavily overrepresents Social well-being (interpersonal relations at work).

External statistics cited: WEF (2020) estimated that 50% of all employees will need re-qualification by 2025; WEF (2022) reported 1 in 5 workers willing to resign ("quiet quitting").

## Test Data / Dataset Source

Systematic literature review: 902 papers from Scopus and Web of Science, March 2020–May 2023. No primary data collected; analysis is entirely of secondary literature.

## Final Result

The joint skills + well-being analysis reveals two structural gaps in the academic literature that do not reflect the realities of the pandemic-era labor market. First, content creation skills — the ability to use AI tools to generate new digital outputs, which became dramatically more demanded during the pandemic — are covered by only 1 of 11 digital skills papers analyzed. Second, psychosomatic and professional well-being are entirely absent from the well-being literature, despite being the most directly impacted dimensions when workers are compelled to rapidly acquire new digital competencies under time pressure.

**What works well here:** The dual-framework approach — using Livingstone's skills model and Van Horn's well-being model simultaneously — forces the analysis to cover both professional and personal impacts of AI adoption, rather than treating them as separate literatures. The bibliometric analysis with VOSviewer produces an evidence-based map of where research attention is concentrated and where it is absent, which is more actionable than a narrative synthesis alone. The identification of content creation as the most underrepresented skill category is a finding with immediate implications for curriculum design: university programs are teaching students to use AI tools but not to produce with them. The "quiet quitting" statistic is contextually well-placed: 1 in 5 employees willing to resign during the pandemic period is a direct signal that well-being impacts are not being adequately addressed by organizations.

## Limitations

The review is temporally bounded to the pandemic period (March 2020–May 2023), which limits generalizability to pre-pandemic and post-pandemic dynamics. Only English-language papers were included, introducing significant geographic and cultural bias toward Western academic contexts. The analysis of 15 skills papers and 35 well-being papers is a limited sample — the funnel reduced 902 papers to these counts, and the selection criteria may have excluded relevant work in adjacent fields (organizational behavior, human-computer interaction, educational technology). The subjective measurement basis of the well-being papers (self-reported surveys) introduces measurement bias that the review acknowledges but cannot correct for. The absence of Professional and Psychosomatic well-being papers from the retrieved literature could reflect either genuine research gaps or the search terms used.

**The applicability limitation:** This paper studies the effects of AI adoption on workers already in organizations — it does not address students preparing to enter the labor market. The skills that organizations are demanding their existing employees develop (upskilling) may differ from the skills students need to acquire to enter those organizations (foundational employability skills). The reskilling literature also focuses heavily on workers displaced by automation, not on students choosing initial career paths.

## Final Summary

This systematic review provides a well-structured evidence base for the dual impact of AI adoption on employee skills and well-being during the pandemic period. Its identification of content creation and psychosomatic well-being as research blind spots is an actionable contribution for both academic researchers and curriculum designers.
