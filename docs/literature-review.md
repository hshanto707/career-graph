# Current Literature Review

Gupta et al. [1] proposed an AI-powered career advisory platform combining collaborative filtering, content-based filtering, and deep learning (TensorFlow/Keras) with NLP-based resume analysis using spaCy and Hugging Face Transformers, alongside a real-time job market dashboard and iterative feedback loop. The architecture is comprehensive and includes algorithmic bias mitigation and GDPR compliance, but the paper reports no quantitative evaluation metrics, making it impossible to assess actual recommendation quality.

José-García et al. [2] built C3-IoC, a deployed career guidance system for UK IT students that matches individual skill profiles to 26 job roles derived from 22,359 job advertisements and the O*NET database, using an asymmetric similarity metric that penalizes gaps but not surpluses and a force-directed network visualization evaluated with 64 real students. The system is a diagnostic tool only — it shows students where they stand in the job space but provides no ordered learning path to reach their target role.

Cramarenco et al. [3] conducted a PRISMA-based systematic review of 103 articles on AI's joint impact on employee skills and well-being during the COVID-19 pandemic, finding that technical skills dominate the reskilling literature while content creation skills are covered by only 1 of 11 papers, and citing WEF projections that 50% of employees will require re-qualification by 2025. The review is limited to workers already in organizations, not students preparing to enter the labor market.

Ladkat and Bharati [4] built a four-agent local LLM pipeline — Profile, Career, Skills, and Roadmap agents — hosted via Ollama, achieving 85% career relevance, 90% skill mapping precision, and sub-20-second latency in a 20-participant study. All career demand signals are drawn from the LLM's training memory, introducing a knowledge-cutoff problem in fast-moving fields and preventing grounding in live market data.

Duc et al. [5] built a ReAct-based LLM agent combining a Playwright crawler, Gemini 2.5 parser, PostgreSQL with pgvector, and four tools — SQL execution, semantic search, visualization, and RAG-based advice — applied to 3,745 Vietnamese IT job postings to demonstrate that tool-augmented agents answer market questions far better than vanilla LLMs. The system operates at the population level and cannot address student-specific queries such as "given my skills, what should I learn next for this role?"

Kavargyris et al. [6] introduced KANVAS, a KAN-based labor market classifier trained on 9,357 job postings that distinguishes traditional AI roles (SAS, DevOps, statistics) from GenAI-facing roles (prompt engineering, LangChain, AI Agents) with 79% accuracy and ROC AUC of 0.86 using SHAP-based interpretability. The system is job-centric and cannot compute a personalized skill gap for a specific student's current profile.

Nikoloski et al. [7] proposed a conceptual Labor Market Intelligence framework for identifying green skill mismatches using the ESCO taxonomy (13,890 skills, 571 labeled green) and defining three mismatch types — shortage, deficit, and obsolescence — that map directly onto what CareerGraph's SkillGapAgent computes. The paper presents no implementation, dataset, or empirical results, limiting it to a research blueprint rather than a validated system.

Majumdar [8] proposed a hybrid skill extraction pipeline combining spaCy for explicit mentions and GPT-4o for inferring implicit skills from contextual phrases, achieving 90–95% career trajectory accuracy on a retrospective validation of 20 historical resumes for mid-career professionals. All role recommendations are drawn from GPT-4o's training memory without connection to live job postings, whereas CareerGraph grounds every output in real ingested market data.

Tang et al. [9] conducted a systematic review of 85 explainability papers in person-job recommendation systems (PRISMA 2020, Krippendorff's α = 0.87), finding that counterfactual explanations achieve the highest composite Explainability-Performance score (0.95) and that hybrid cross-layer methods achieve ≥20% explainability gains at ≤10% accuracy loss. This directly motivates CareerGraph's combination of PathFinderAgent KG-path reasoning with ReasoningAgent natural language output.

de Groot et al. [10] constructed a Skills and Occupation Knowledge Graph from ISCO and ESCO taxonomies enriched with co-occurrence patterns from 600,000 Dutch job postings, applying Jaccard distance for career pathfinding via Dijkstra's algorithm and Node2Vec for link prediction — where Node2Vec outperforms Preferential Attachment at realistic negative-to-positive ratios of 3:1 or greater. The graph contains no student node, so it cannot compute a personalized gap for a specific individual.

Weichselbraun et al. [11] built the CareerCoach system, a continuing education knowledge graph with 73,969 nodes and 734,447 edges from 488 Swiss education provider websites, whose career path recommender computes relative skill gap as |target \ user| / |target| — the clearest published precedent for CareerGraph's SkillGapAgent formula — and deployed it in production across three commercial companies. User profiles are computed on the fly and not persisted across sessions, preventing any tracking of individual progress.

Sujitha et al. [12] proposed an ML-powered skill gap pipeline using spaCy and BERT NER for resume parsing, Jaccard and cosine similarity for gap detection, and the Skill Gap Index (SGI = [(Required − Acquired) / Required] × 100) and Proficiency Match Rate (PMR = [Matched / Total Required] × 100) as summary metrics that are direct precedents for CareerGraph's readiness score. The paper presents no empirical evaluation and treats all skills as equally weighted, which CareerGraph improves by applying importance weights and proficiency bonuses via HAS_SKILL edge properties in Neo4j.

Ramazanova et al. [13] constructed a three-domain knowledge graph in Neo4j linking university educational programs, hh.kz job vacancies, and Coursera courses, with a multilingual sentence transformer (paraphrase-multilingual-mpnet-base-v2) achieving F1 = 0.8917 for skill paraphrase classification — outperforming BERT (0.6988) and Word2Vec (0.7731) — and six Cypher query scenarios implementing gap identification and course recommendations. There is no learner node, so gap identification operates at the curriculum level and all students from the same program receive identical recommendations.

Tavakoli et al. [14] built eDoer, evaluated through a rigorous 156-participant RCT, showing that quality-filtered market-aligned OER significantly outperforms self-directed search (p < 0.001) using 21,937 Monster.com vacancies and LDA-based decomposition of six skills into 132 learning topics. Critically, content-format personalization adds no benefit (p = 0.892), motivating CareerGraph's gap-grounded personalization based on actual skill deficits rather than format preferences.

Sun et al. [15] presented SeSRDQN, formulating long-term skill acquisition as a multi-objective RL problem with salary and difficulty rewards, achieving an average salary of 38.24K RMB at a 20-step horizon against a greedy baseline of 35.09K and GPT-4's 13.66K, while the self-explaining extension raised user Trust scores from 3.14 to 4.19 across a 48-participant study. The 61.9-second per-prototype overhead and proprietary Chinese dataset motivate CareerGraph's use of deterministic BFS and topological sort over LEADS_TO edges as a lighter-weight alternative.

Ashrafi et al. [16] presented Career-gAIde, combining a CNN-based salary classifier (70.70% accuracy across 10 income tiers) with a weighted similarity function that rewards jobs above the user's current salary band, and achieving recall-optimized skill gap identification with F1 = 0.83 for required skills and F1 = 0.78 for deficiencies. The limitation is a static 8,870-job corpus with no re-ingestion mechanism and a book-only learning path with no ordering semantics.

Gnanapriya and Athira [17] presented Skill-Pulse, integrating seasonality-aware demand forecasting, Isolation Forest anomaly detection for volatile skill segments, Dijkstra-based career path optimization, and semantic resume parsing in a single Streamlit platform, evaluated on a simulated Indian metro dataset for five IT roles. The platform targets institutional stakeholders rather than individual students and cannot account for a specific student's current skill position or proficiency levels.

Vultureanu-Albisi et al. [18] proposed XR²K²G, a five-stage pipeline — KG construction, TransE embeddings, GAT teacher, Knowledge Distillation to a student MLP, and Q-learning with LIME and SHAP explanation — achieving precision = 0.80 and NDCG = 1.00 on a single representative test user. The post-hoc explanations operate on compressed student embeddings rather than the original KG structure, creating a chain-of-explanation problem where each compression step degrades faithfulness.

Qin et al. [19] built DuerQues, an interview question generation and recommendation system using distantly supervised NER (F1 = 0.93) and an RGCN-based recommendation module (AUC = 0.93, Hit@10 = 0.84) built on a skill co-occurrence graph derived from Chinese search engine click-through logs. Though the system targets interview preparation rather than career guidance, its skill co-occurrence graph architecture directly informs the LEADS_TO edge structure in CareerGraph's Neo4j knowledge graph.

Fettach et al. [20] introduced T-JobEdKG, a temporal knowledge graph from 99,676 Moroccan job postings (2005–2022) and 10,180 Coursera courses, where their TA-DistMult model — using an LSTM to encode evolving job-skill relationships — achieved Hit@1 = 61.77%, Hit@3 = 84.53%, and Hit@10 = 95.08%, substantially outperforming diachronic embedding models. The key insight is that skill-to-job relationships evolve over time while skill entity semantics remain stable, making time-aware relation embedding the correct architecture for temporal demand forecasting.
